# Patterns

One tell proves nothing. Most signs matter in combination. Examples below are invented.

## 1. Not X, but Y

It sets up a denial the reader never held.

- Before: "It's not about speed, it's about trust."
- After: "Customers stayed because refunds arrived in two days." (Only if that is in the source.)
- Rule: state the true part. Drop the denial.

## 2. Groups of three

Three parallel items used for rhythm, not because there are three things.

- Before: "We build fast, scale smoothly and ship securely."
- After: keep only the item the source supports. "We shipped the login fix in one day."

## 3. Setup before the point

- Before: "Here's the thing: most teams skip testing."
- After: "Most teams skip testing." Or cut it if the source does not say so.

## 4. Fake meaning on the tail

A trailing "-ing" clause that claims importance with no fact behind it.

- Before: "The update reduced load time, highlighting our commitment to quality."
- After: "The update cut load time from 4 seconds to 2." (Only with a real number.)

## 5. Puffed importance

Legacy, pivotal-moment and testament language.

- Before: "This marks a pivotal moment in the company's journey."
- After: say what happened and when. "The company opened its second office in March."

## 6. Stacked fragments

- Before: "One tool. One team. Zero excuses."
- After: one plain sentence. Flag only when three or more repeat in a row. A caller's voice file can allow them.

## 7. Closer that repeats or flatters

A last line that restates the text or praises the reader, and recap paragraphs ("In conclusion", "Overall").

- Before: "Whatever you build, remember: the best tool is the one you use."
- After: end on the last concrete point or the next step.

## 8. Piled-up hedges

- Before: "This may potentially help in some cases."
- After: "This helped in two of five tests." Or say you do not know.

## 9. Vague sources

"Experts agree", "studies show", "many believe".

- After: name the source and date, or cut the claim.

## 10. Rotating synonyms

One thing called a "client", then a "partner", then a "stakeholder".

- Rule: use the same word for the same thing.

## 11. False ranges

- Before: "From startups to enterprises."
- After: name the actual customers or drop it.

## 12. Same shape everywhere

Every section the same length, the same order, the same punchline. Headings from one formula ("The Power of...", "The Danger of..."). Bold lead-in plus colon on every bullet. Emoji on bullets or headings.

- Rule: let each section be as long as its content. Use the content's own words for headings.

## 13. Challenge-then-optimism formula

"Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive." It outlines challenges, then ends on a vague positive.

- After: name the challenge and what was done. Or cut.

## 14. Generic where a fact should be

Specific facts replaced by positive, general wording.

- Before: "a revolutionary leader in its field."
- After: the specific fact from the source. If the source has none, say less.

Related: content that looks polished but gives the reader nothing. Test: after reading the text, can you name one thing you now know or can do that you could not before?

## 15. Chatbot leftovers

The most objective signs: collaborative phrases ("I hope this helps", "Certainly!"), "As an AI language model", knowledge-cutoff disclaimers, unfilled placeholders, Markdown pasted where it shows raw, and tool residue in links or citations such as `utm_source=chatgpt.com` or `turn0search0`.

- Rule: always remove. Never fill a placeholder with an invented value. Flag it.

## 16. Sameness across a batch

Five posts from one run that open with the same kind of line, run the same length and end the same way.

- Rule: compare openers, lengths and endings across the batch. Change the weakest repeats. Do not add new facts to do it.

## 17. Fixes that make new tells

- Swapping "leverage" for "harness". Same tell, new word.
- Replacing long dashes with a colon in every sentence.
- Turning every sentence short to avoid rhythm. That makes stacked fragments.
- Adding an invented detail to sound human. That makes invented content.

## 18. Padding

Sentences that give the reader nothing new: the question restated, "here is what I will cover", a summary of what was just said, background the reader already has.

- Test: if the sentence were cut, what would the reader lose? If the answer is nothing, flag it. A long text is not padding. Length alone proves nothing.
- Before: "Thanks for asking about the refund. A refund is when money is returned to you. We have issued your refund of 40 euros. In summary, your refund of 40 euros has been issued."
- After: "We issued your refund of 40 euros." (Keep every fact the original had.)
- Rule: cut the sentence, not the fact. Do not add content to fill the space.
